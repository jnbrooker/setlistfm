A model of touring decisions · setlist.fm + Pollstar

Why tours skip your city

A city wants to know whether building an arena would bring more shows. The question is causal; the only evidence is what promoters already did. This is how the model bridges that gap, what it can prove, and exactly where it stops.

Part one · the mechanism

Cities compete for the same night

When a tour books four Italian dates it is not grading each city on its own merits. It is picking from a menu, and the cities on that menu are rivals. So the model is a conditional logit: every city gets a score built from its attributes, and those scores become probabilities that must sum to one.

P(city j) = e^(xj'β) ÷ Σₕ e^(xₖ'β)   over every city k on the menu

The denominator does the work. Because the probabilities are forced to add to one, a city can only gain by taking from the others. There is no way for everyone to win, which is exactly right: a tour has a fixed number of nights.

Why everything about the artist disappears

Look at the formula again. The numerator and denominator both contain the artist. Their popularity, budget, booking agent and fanbase are identical whichever city they choose, so they cancel completely out of the ratio.

That sounds like throwing away information. It is the single best property of the model. It means the coefficients are identified from within-tour variation only: not “do big acts play big cities” — obviously they do — but given that this tour played four Italian cities, why those four and not the other thirty. Every confounder attached to the act is differenced away without anyone having to measure it.

The price is that an artist trait can only enter by being interacted with a city trait. That is why the model carries a term for room big enough × act size: it asks whether capacity binds harder for bigger acts. Switch the act above to 18,000 and then to 3,500, and watch how much the room matters in each case.

Part two · the difficulty

Arenas get built where the demand already was

Here is the problem that makes this hard. Plot every European market's show count against the size of its biggest indoor room and you get a strong, clean relationship. It is tempting to read the slope as “what a bigger room buys you”.

It is not. Cities that build arenas are cities where promoters already expected demand. The room and the shows have a common cause, and the slope contains both. Press the button to hold catchment constant and watch what happens to it.

One control removes 56% of it. And catchment is only the most obvious confounder — a strong local promoter, a university, a motorway junction and a habit of selling out are all unmeasured and all point the same way.

So the model is fitted twice, and the honest answer is the interval between:

A lets capacity keep credit for every unobserved reason a city is attractive, so it is an upper bound. B adds how many shows the city already hosts — the best available proxy for those unobserved reasons, but also partly the thing capacity delivers — so it is a lower bound. The truth sits between. This data cannot say where.

The row that survives is the finding. General size is explained away by how busy a city already was, which is what you would expect if it were mostly selection. Whether the room clears this particular act's bar is not explained away. That distinction is the whole reason the model asks about a threshold rather than about size.

Part three · a trap worth seeing

The 13.8 million people who were not there

Catchment is the most important control in the model, so measuring it badly poisons everything. The obvious measure — count everyone within 60 km — is badly wrong, and wrong in a way that looks perfectly reasonable.

A 60 km circle around Guildford contains 13.8 million people, because London is forty kilometres up the road. Ranked that way, Guildford is one of the most under-served markets in Europe. It is not. It is a suburb of the best-served one.

Market

The fix is a Voronoi allocation: assign every postcode district to the single nearest market that actually hosts shows, then add them up. Nobody is counted twice, and a town in a big city's shadow keeps only the people for whom it genuinely is the closest option.

It is the simplest defensible model of competition and it explains in one sentence: people go to their nearest market. A gravity model would capture that a bigger city pulls from further away, at the cost of a distance-decay parameter nobody can observe and a number no reader could check by hand. Not worth it here.

The known cost, stated rather than hidden: this understates big cities slightly and overstates small ones, because people do sometimes travel past a small market to reach a large one. Both figures are kept so either reading is available.

Part four · how far each number reaches

Four layers, kept deliberately apart

Each layer is more ambitious and less certain than the one below. They are never mixed into a single figure, so you can always see how far out on the limb any given number sits.

01Counting“what happens today”

The capacity ladder, which tours skipped a city, whether room size was actually the obstacle, and how the city compares with others of its size. No model at all — every figure is a count, a maximum or a median of something that really happened.

Probably most of the value. If the descriptive picture shows no gap, nothing above it will manufacture one.

02The choice model“this associates with that”

The conditional logit above, fitted on 65,288 choice occasions from 6,690 tours across seven countries, with standard errors clustered by tour.

It genuinely predicts. On tours held out of the fitting, it ranks the real choice first 38.8% of the time against a 1.7% random baseline, on menus averaging 58 cities — and out-of-sample fit (0.320) barely trails in-sample (0.329), so it is not overfitting.

It still cannot say a room causes shows.

03The natural experiment“this caused that”

Stop comparing cities with each other; compare a city with its own past. 46 markets gained a room of 8,000+ between 2014 and 2024. What happened to them, measured against cities where nothing opened?

Whatever made a city attractive was already there the year before its arena opened, so the selection story has no purchase on this comparison — provided the two groups were moving together beforehand. Testing that is the next section.

04Simulation“here is the spread”

Redrawing the coefficients from their covariance to put a band on any figure. Worth having, and the narrowest uncertainty in the project: it covers sampling error only, not whether capacity causes anything. A tight band here means the sample is large, not that the answer is safe.

Part five · getting the experiment right

Why the obvious estimator gives the wrong answer

Arenas do not all open in the same year, and that innocuous fact breaks the standard tool. The usual approach — a regression with city and year fixed effects — quietly compares every pair of cities, including pairs where the “control” is a city that already got its arena.

If the effect of a room grows over time, those comparisons subtract a rising trend that is itself a treatment effect. The estimate is dragged down — and can even flip sign while every individual effect is positive.

Toggle below to see which cells each method is willing to use as a control.

Figures from a simulation with a known answer, built to test the estimator. With a constant effect both methods are fine. With an effect that grows after opening — the realistic case — the group-time estimator returns +9.21 against TWFE's +6.87. The bias is not small and it is not random; it is structural.

Part six · what actually happened

46 cities that gained a room

Each bar is the effect on a city's tour-visits, by years since its room opened, against cities where nothing opened. The bars left of zero are the test that decides everything.

The whole design rests on an assumption that cannot be checked directly: absent the new room, treated and control cities would have moved in parallel. What can be checked is whether they were moving in parallel beforehand. If treated cities were already pulling away, the room was built because the city was taking off — and that is the selection story all over again.

Extra tour-visits a year. The leads are not flat: treated cities were already gaining +0.34 visits a year before their rooms opened, so roughly half the raw effect was a trend already running.

Removing it is a sensitivity, not a correction. Extrapolating the pre-trend assumes it would have continued — exactly as unverifiable as the parallel-trends assumption it patches. Read +1.27 and +2.49 as the two ends of a range, and note that the whole range sits at the bottom of what Layer 2 alone would have suggested. That is what you would expect if the cross-section was inflated by selection — and it is the closest thing here to a result confirming itself.

Half the calendarOnly multi-city tours involve a choice between cities. A one-off local gig had no menu, so there is nothing to model. Across Italy that is 49% of shows — 61% in Bari, 9% in Sanremo, whose year is one festival.
The building itselfSightlines, loading bays, rail links, the promoter and the rent are invisible. A better room of the same capacity scores identically.
A second room the same sizeThe model works through the ceiling, so it registers as nothing at all. Date congestion is real and unseen.
